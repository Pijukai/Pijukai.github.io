BRAIN Project

Work in progress

Decoding what a person sees, and hears, from non-invasive brain recordings, and bringing it close enough to real time for a live demonstration. A team research project, in which I worked on live image decoding with an EEG foundation model and on mixing images and music in the multimodal pipeline.

  • EEG
  • Foundation models
  • LoRA
  • CLIP / CLAP
  • Diffusion
  • NeuroAI
Objective
Reconstruct the image or sound a person perceives from EEG recordings, which are highly noisy, subject-dependent multi-channel time series, fast enough for a real-time demonstrator.
Result
An EEG-to-image pipeline running end to end in 747 ms, adapting to a new person in about 7 minutes from a fraction of the usual recordings, with competitive retrieval performance.

Context

EEG (electroencephalography) records brain activity through electrodes on the scalp: cheap, safe and non-invasive, it can reveal what someone is looking at, listening to or feeling. But the signal is very noisy and differs from one person to the next. Decoding an image typically needs hours of recordings from each person and a long adaptation of the model, which rules out a live demo. And decoding sounds, or images and sounds together, is still largely unexplored.

We set out to do three things:

  • Bring image decoding close to real time, without hours of recordings or long adaptation for each new person.
  • Decode music and multimodal stimuli (image + sound), beyond the usual visual-only setting.
  • Bring an out-of-service EEG setup back to life, then build the software around it to run the whole system live.

We used THINGS-EEG2 for vision: 10 participants, 64 channels, 16,740 naturalistic object images across 1,674 concepts, plus 200 held-out test concepts. For music we used NMED-T and NMED-H: 14 songs heard by up to 48 listeners. We harmonised everything onto a common 64-channel layout at 250 Hz, matching our own live EEG cap.

Progress is measured with four proxy metrics: decoding accuracy, end-to-end latency, adaptation time for a new person, and the amount of recording that person needs to give.

Part 1 · Decoding images, music, and both

The first half of the semester focused on decoding accuracy, including for modalities that are rarely tackled.

1 Vision: EEG → CLIP space → image

A transformer encoder (ATM-S), trained separately for each person, maps one EEG trial into CLIP’s image–text embedding space, close to the image that caused it. It learns contrastively: pulled towards the right image, pushed away from all others. Because EEG only carries a coarse trace of a scene, we trained it under an uncertainty-aware blur prior: the target image is blurred in proportion to how unreliably the brain encoded it, so the model isn’t punished for detail the signal never had. This clearly improved on the vanilla encoder.

The decoded vector is then used to retrieve the closest test image, or to reconstruct one: a small prior network pulls it back onto the manifold of real image embeddings, and an IP-Adapter feeds it to Stable Diffusion XL in place of a text prompt.

The best subject (a 1.7 M-parameter decoder) beats the subject-averaged results of ENIGMA and ATM-S on retrieval. Reconstruction fidelity trails ENIGMA, though (CLIP 67.8 vs 81.5, PixCorr 0.118 vs 0.172): the decoder is parameter-efficient and strong at retrieval rather than the sharpest reconstructor. Averaged over subjects it reaches a Top-1 of 24.2 % (± 5.2).

2 Music: EEG → which song is playing

A convolutional encoder (SampleCNN2DEEG, from the PredANN line of work) reads a 3-second EEG window as an image of channels × time and predicts which song the person is hearing. An optional contrastive head can align it with CLAP captions of each song (harmony, timbre, rhythm, mood, genre) written by Music Flamingo.

On NMED-T this is on par with the published baseline (0.823) and far above our own first attempt (0.635). Regenerating the music itself from the predicted caption with AudioLDM2 remains weak.

3 Image + music together

The multimodal pipeline turns both decodings into words. The visual embedding retrieves an image caption (object, attribute, scene, theme) and the predicted song retrieves its music caption, and the two are merged into one prompt. The image is generated from that prompt and warm-started from the visual embedding through an IP-Adapter. A weight (ip_scale) balances the two paths, and the same music caption drives a 3-second audio clip.

At ip_scale = 0.8 the visual embedding dominates: the fused prompt scores the same as the visual-only one (CLIP 0.326 vs 0.329, two-way accuracy 0.655 for both), and the music only reshapes the image at lower weights. The output is coherent, with the song’s identity well carried but its waveform still hard to reconstruct (CLAP 0.31, up to 0.43 when averaging several generations). This is a first step: its full potential needs paired image + audio recordings.

Part 2 · Toward a real-time demonstrator

In the second half we optimised all four metrics at once. The standard recipe (an EEG decoder followed by SDXL) has a bottleneck at each end: the decoder needs hours of recordings and a long adaptation for every new person, and the image generator dominates the latency.

1 An EEG foundation model that adapts in minutes

Fast adaptation calls for a strong shared backbone plus a tiny person-specific part. We used the LUNA EEG foundation model with LoRA adapters, prepared in three phases:

  1. Continuous learning: extending LUNA’s pre-training to THINGS-EEG2, following its original recipe.
  2. CLIP alignment: freezing the backbone and training a new attention layer to bring its representations into CLIP space.
  3. Subject adaptation: the model stays frozen and only a LoRA adapter (rank 8) learns the new person, sped up by three refinements: PiSSA initialisation from the weights’ principal components, rsLoRA scaling (α/√r) for steadier training, and LoRA+, which gives one adapter matrix an 8× higher learning rate.

The person used for evaluation (subject 10) was held out of phases 1 and 2, so adaptation is tested on a truly new brain.

~7 minto adapt to a new person (25 % of the data, 10 epochs), vs 59 min with everything

2 A generator fast enough for real time

We benchmarked two families of accelerators against 50-step SDXL, with the same input embeddings so the generator was the only variable: distillation (DMD2, SDXL-Lightning, SDXL-Turbo), which squeezes the denoising into a few steps, and training-free caching (DeepCache), which reuses deep activations between neighbouring steps.

Caching cuts latency 2.5× with almost no loss of fidelity, while distillation is faster still but drifts further from the reference. Stacking the two compounds their approximations and collapses quality, so you have to pick one. DeepCache is the best trade-off for reconstruction quality, and the live pipeline uses one-step SDXL-Turbo to stay well under a second.

3 The final pipeline

The full pipeline runs in 747 ms end to end, under the real-time threshold. With all the data it lands slightly below ENIGMA and ATM-S but stays competitive. With only 10 % of the data it matches the full setting on every reconstruction metric (only retrieval drops), and the 25 %, 10-epoch “live” setting stays competitive too.

From offline to live

We didn’t build new hardware: we took an EEG acquisition setup that was out of service and hadn’t been used in a long time, and made it work again: a 64-channel g.GAMMAcap feeding four synchronised g.USBamp amplifiers. We then built the software around it. We read them through an open-source USB path (libusb and mushu) inside a Linux VM, bypassing the manufacturer’s closed Windows driver, and timestamped EEG and stimulus markers on a single Lab Streaming Layer clock. A live epoch has exactly the shape of an offline one (training montage, 250 Hz, 240-sample windows), so the models can consume it as-is. We confirmed real, time-locked EEG with a clean eyes-closed alpha rhythm.

The remaining obstacle is signal quality, not software. Lab-grade training data versus our cap (impedances, line noise), the legacy channel ordering, and the much higher signal-to-noise needed for visual responses still stand between us and a valid live reconstruction. The next steps are calibration and the ~7-minute on-device adaptation characterised offline.

Limits & next steps

The recording each new person must give still needs to shrink: generating synthetic EEG is a promising lead. Robustness is the other open question: THINGS-EEG2 is highly curated, and real-world recordings will be noisier.

My role

I worked on two parts of the project:

  • Real-time image decoding. I worked on the LUNA EEG foundation model with LoRA adapters and CLIP alignment, used to decode images for the live demonstrator. I fine-tuned, optimised and benchmarked it as a replacement for task-specific EEG decoders, aiming for real-time speed without giving up accuracy.
  • Multimodal decoding. I designed the prompt composer, which turns the decoded image and music into one unified prompt, and worked out how to mix the two modalities: balancing the visual embedding against the combined text prompt when generating the image.

The real-time work led to an end-to-end EEG-to-image demo with under one second of latency. It retains around 70 % of the performance across metrics (top-k retrieval, SSIM, PixCorr…) while using as little as 25 % of the data and a fifth of the training time to adapt to a new person.
