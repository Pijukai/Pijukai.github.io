Dark Matter Density Generation

Efficient, physically aware generation of dark-matter distributions in galaxy clusters. A team project with Mattéo Santini and Louis James Vasseur.

  • Diffusion Transformer
  • Flow matching
  • AstroPT
  • Contrastive learning
  • Astrophysics
Objective
Generate realistic galaxy-cluster mass maps directly from physical parameters, instead of running new, expensive particle simulations.
Result
Maps in minutes instead of days of simulation, with near-perfect reconstruction of large-scale structure (cross-correlation 0.8–1.0) and a power spectrum within ±10 % of real maps at mid scales.

Context

About 85 % of the matter in the universe is dark matter: we can’t see it, but its gravity shapes everything around it. Galaxy clusters, 1014–1015 times the mass of the Sun and mostly dark matter, bend the light of galaxies behind them. Those distortions make clusters natural “cosmic lenses” to map where dark matter is.

Neural networks are promising for reading physical properties off these maps, such as how strongly dark matter interacts with itself (its self-interaction cross-section, σDM/m). But they need lots of training data, and each new map comes from huge numerical simulations (2 × 1024³ particles) that take days to run. Our question: can a generative model learn to produce realistic cluster maps directly, and skip the simulation?

We worked with 14,400 annotated maps from the BAHAMAS simulations, labelled with the cluster’s total mass, the shape of its brightest galaxy and σDM/m ∈ {0.01, 0.1, 0.3, 1.0} cm²/g. Although σDM/m only takes four values here, we treated it as continuous, since it is a continuous physical quantity and the model should generalise as more data becomes available.

Approach

The pipeline compresses the physics into an embedding, then a diffusion model turns that embedding into a map. There are two ways to get the embedding:

1 Fine-tuning AstroPT into a physics-aware encoder

AstroPT is an autoregressive transformer pretrained on 8.6 M galaxy images, and one of the main foundation models in astrophysics. We fully unfroze it and trained it on two objectives at once, so its embeddings would capture mass and σDM/m well:

  • Regression: a linear head predicts mass, σDM/m, galaxy shape and ellipticity with an MSE loss.
  • Supervised contrastive loss (InfoNCE) on σDM/m: maps with the same value are pulled together and different values pushed apart, giving the diffusion model a well-structured conditioning space. Its weight is warmed up over the first epochs:

λc(e) = λc* · min(1, e / Nw)

2 Flow matching to generate embeddings from physics

To create a new map there is no input image to encode, so we learned to generate embeddings directly from (mass, σDM/m). A small MLP learns a conditioned, time-dependent velocity field vθ that carries Gaussian noise x0 to a point x1 on the embedding manifold, trained with the conditional flow-matching loss:

𝔼t, x₁, x ‖ vθ(x) − ut(x | x₁) ‖²

A grid search over three probability paths (optimal transport, independent coupling, variance preserving) and σmin ∈ [0.001, 1.5] picked the variance-preserving path with σmin = 1:

μt = cos(πt⁄2) · x0 + sin(πt⁄2) · x1

3 A Diffusion Transformer to paint the map

The maps themselves come from a denoising diffusion model (DDPM). Training gradually adds noise to real maps and teaches the network to predict that noise:

xt = √ᾱt · x0 + √(1 − ᾱt) · ε,    loss = ‖ ε − εθ(xt, t) ‖²

The backbone is a Diffusion Transformer (DiT), which improves on U-Net baselines in almost every setting, adapted to our map size. It uses 12 DiT blocks with 8 attention heads each: smaller models never produced density-map patterns, and larger ones overfit or didn’t fit in the cluster’s memory. The embedding conditions every block through Adaptive LayerNorm.

Results

A better encoder

Linear probes on the embeddings show that fine-tuning keeps mass well encoded and makes the self-interaction cross-section much easier to recover:

R² ≈ 0.9mass prediction, before and after fine-tuning (slope 0.91)
0.69 → 0.85recovery of σDM/m = 1.0, baseline → fine-tuned, with tighter uncertainty

The lower σDM/m values still overlap, likely because such weak self-interaction barely changes how a cluster looks.

Generated embeddings

Mass is reproduced faithfully. Generated embeddings score higher than real ones on σDM/m, which sounds good but suggests the simple MLP doesn’t cover the full AstroPT manifold: its samples are a little too “predictable”. That also explains why it is so sensitive to the path and σmin.

Mode 1: generating new maps from physics

The generated maps are coherent and not overly noisy. Since they are new samples rather than copies, we compared them to real maps statistically:

The power spectrum matches within ±10 % at mid scales, with slightly too little power at large scales and too much at the finest scales. Pixel values, skewness and peak counts overlap strongly with real maps, so the model captures the non-Gaussian structure of clusters, but generated maps come out systematically a bit brighter (higher total flux). That is expected: from mass and σDM/m alone, the model can’t know the cluster’s exact geometry (e.g. ellipticity).

Mode 2: reconstructing existing maps

Reconstruction is near-perfect for the overall structure (r between 0.8 and 1.0), solid for finer details (0.3–0.6), and, as expected from a stochastic model, weaker for the very finest grain. The log-ratio of power spectra stays near 0 at every frequency, so reconstructed and original maps carry the same structure across scales.

Limits & responsible use

A generated map can look realistic and still break physics, and unlike a chatbot’s mistake, that is hard to spot without domain expertise. It could mislead downstream research on dark matter. We publish validation metrics with every result, recommend staying within the trained range (σDM/m ∈ {0.01, 0.1, 0.3, 1.0} cm²/g), and advise caution with Mode 1, which is less accurate than reconstruction.

Each part (fine-tuning, flow matching, diffusion) can still be improved, and the approach could be extended to X-ray and stellar-mass maps.

My role

I built the generative heart of the pipeline: the diffusion model that turns an embedding into a density map. I implemented the Diffusion Transformer within the DDPM framework, adapted it to our maps so it predicts only the noise, and conditioned every block on the parameter embeddings through Adaptive LayerNorm. I also sized the model to 12 blocks and 8 heads: big enough for density-map patterns to appear, small enough not to overfit and to fit in the cluster’s memory.

The result is maps generated in minutes instead of the 5–7 days a numerical simulation takes, with a cross-correlation of 0.8–1.0 at large scales and a log power-spectrum ratio near zero across all frequencies against the original maps.
