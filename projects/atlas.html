CERN · Sep 2025 — Feb 2026

ATLAS Project

Fast, machine-learning simulation of particle showers in the ATLAS calorimeter at CERN’s Large Hadron Collider. I led this EPFL AI Team project, in partnership with CERN.

  • Generative models
  • Stochastic interpolants
  • Consistency distillation
  • Transformers
  • Particle physics
Objective
Replace slow Monte Carlo simulation of calorimeter showers with generative models that are much faster, while staying accurate.
Result
Results close to the established state of the art while using less memory: stochastic interpolants for low-resolution showers and a consistency-distilled EDM model for high-resolution ones.

Context

When a particle flies into dense matter, it sets off a chain reaction: it radiates, splits into new particles, and those split again. This cascade is a particle shower. ATLAS’s calorimeter measures the energy each shower deposits in its cells, which is how physicists read the energy of what came out of a collision.

To interpret real data, physicists need huge numbers of simulated showers. The reference tool, Geant4, follows every single interaction with Monte Carlo methods. It is the most accurate approach, and also the most time-consuming: simulation is a major bottleneck for the LHC.

Setup & goals

We worked with electromagnetic showers, started by photons, using a universal, high-granularity representation: the shower is described on a cylinder aligned with the incoming particle, cut into layers along its depth, radial slices and angular segments. Because it doesn’t depend on one detector’s exact cell layout, the same model can serve different detectors.

That makes the data very high-dimensional, and the goals pull in different directions:

  • a better accuracy / speed trade-off than existing fast simulators
  • generative models that handle high-dimensional data
  • detector-agnostic methods
  • expressive models that remain compact in memory and fast at inference

Approach

1 Stochastic interpolants for low-resolution showers

Stochastic interpolants build a bridge between two distributions, here a simple latent distribution and real showers, and learn the velocity that carries one to the other. Generation then integrates that learned flow:

xt = α(t) · x0 + β(t) · x1 + γ(t) · z,    dXt/dt = b(t, Xt)

The framework is flexible: we can choose the latent distribution and the interpolation path, and the bridge works in both directions. We paired it with a transformer backbone. The challenge was the complex shower space: keeping the speed/accuracy balance while keeping the model small.

2 A distilled diffusion model for high resolution

For high-resolution showers we used an EDM diffusion model, accurate but slow because it denoises over many steps. We then distilled it with continuous-time consistency distillation, following the SANA-Sprint framework: a student learns to jump straight to the result the teacher reaches step by step, guided by a self-consistency loss and an adversarial (GAN) loss from discriminator heads.

  • Fewer diffusion steps, so much faster inference
  • More stable distillation than earlier methods
  • Accuracy that holds independently of the noise level

The hard parts were distilling through such high-dimensional data, the complexity of the framework, and managing large models.

3 A third direction explored by the team: Hamiltonian flows

In parallel, other team members explored fixed-kinetic Neural Hamiltonian Flows coupled with a VAE. They are volume-preserving, need fewer integration steps, and fix the kinetic term, leaving fewer parameters to learn. The catch is that they double the dimensionality and need better conditioning to scale.

Results

The resulting models reached results close to the established state of the art for fast calorimeter simulation while using less memory: stochastic interpolants for low-resolution showers, and the consistency-distilled EDM model for high resolution, aiming at faster inference.

My role

I was Project Lead and ML Engineer, leading a team of three within the EPFL AI Team in partnership with CERN. On the technical side, I worked on accelerating Geant4-based Monte Carlo calorimeter simulation with generative ML: stochastic interpolants for low-resolution showers, and an EDM model distilled through continuous-time consistency distillation for high-resolution ones, reaching results close to the state of the art with a smaller memory footprint.
